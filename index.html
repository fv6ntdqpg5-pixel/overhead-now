Your location and filters

Every tracked, active satellite, propagated from its published orbital elements to this second. Pick any spot on Earth, or isolate one country's fleet. Then go outside, or check the same pass on Heavens-Above, N2YO, or NASA's Spot the Station.

Elements from CelesTrak, snapshot –. Propagation is SGP4, the same model every tracking site uses. Positions drift a few kilometers per day after the snapshot, which is why pass times still land within seconds for a week or more.

On the globe: drag to rotate; pinch, scroll or use the slider to zoom. In 3D the shaded side of the Earth is where it is night right now, from the sun's actual position. To use your own spot, press "Tap the globe to set location" and tap it; the horizon list, sky chart, and ISS passes all update. Zoom out to about 7 and the geostationary ring appears 35,786 km above the equator, exactly where every satellite dish is aimed. Switch to the South Pole view: polar-orbit satellites cross Antarctica every few minutes.

Your sky right now

Zenith at center, horizon at the edge, north up. The dashed green arc is the geostationary belt: every TV dish in your town points at it. The largest dot is the ISS when it's up. Tap a dot to pick it; its path across your sky for the 15 minutes either side of now is drawn as a line. A picked satellite that is below your horizon is not on this chart; the readout says so.

Dish test: one satellite, six latitudes

GOES-19 sits over 75.2°W and never moves in the sky. From each city below, here is the angle above the horizon a dish must point to see it, computed live from its elements. On a flat plane the angle would fall off slowly with distance and never reach zero. On a sphere it hits the horizon near 81° latitude. Every satellite TV installer's tables agree with these angles. Each dish below is drawn at its computed tilt; the dashed level line is the horizon.
